How to Draw House Plans (And When to Get Them Drawn for You)

A step-by-step guide to drawing your own house plans to scale — measuring, walls, rooms, doors and furniture — and when it makes sense to get professional help.

By Muhammad Kamran · October 27, 2026 · 8 min read

Drawing your own house plans is a great way to work out what you want before you spend money on design and construction. You do not need special software to start — a pencil, graph paper and a tape measure go a long way. This guide walks through how to draw house plans step by step, and explains when it makes sense to have them drawn for you.

Step 1: Measure the plot or the existing house

Every good plan starts with real measurements.

  • For a new house, write down the plot size (for example 30x60 ft, 50x90 ft, 10 marla or 1 kanal), which side faces the road, and where north is.
  • For a renovation, measure each room wall by wall, and note the positions of doors, windows, stairs and plumbing.
  • Check local rules. Setbacks (how far the house must stay from the plot edges), height limits and parking rules decide how much space you actually have to build on.

Write every measurement down. A plan is only as reliable as the numbers behind it.

Step 2: Choose a scale

Drawing at scale means every line on paper stands for a fixed real distance. Common choices are:

  • 1/4 inch = 1 foot on standard graph paper, or
  • 1:100 if you work in metric (1 cm on paper = 1 m in reality).

Mark the scale in a corner of the sheet so anyone reading the plan understands it.

Step 3: Draw the outer walls

Start with the outline of the house. Draw the outer walls as double lines to show their thickness — exterior walls are commonly around 8–9 inches (200–230 mm) thick, depending on the construction method. Leave openings where the main doors will go.

Step 4: Divide the space into rooms

Now draw the inner walls. Think about how your family moves through the house:

  • Keep the kitchen close to the dining area and, ideally, near the entrance for groceries.
  • Put bedrooms away from the noisiest spaces.
  • Make sure every bedroom can reach a bathroom without walking through another bedroom.
  • Allow space for the stairs if the house has more than one floor — stairs take more room than most people expect.

Write the room name and size inside each room, for example "Bedroom 12' x 14'".

Step 5: Add doors and windows

Draw doors with an arc showing which way they swing, so you can check they do not hit furniture or each other. Draw windows as thin lines in the wall. Think about light: in most homes, living spaces benefit from the sunniest side of the plot.

Step 6: Test the furniture

This is the step most people skip, and the one that catches the most mistakes. Cut small paper shapes for beds, sofas, dining tables and cars at the same scale, and place them on the plan. Can you walk around the bed? Does the dining table for six actually fit? Will the car door open in the garage?

Step 7: Repeat for each floor

Draw each floor on a separate sheet at the same scale, and make sure stairs, columns and bathrooms line up between floors. Stacking bathrooms above each other keeps plumbing simpler and cheaper.

Free tools if you prefer a computer

If you would rather draw digitally, free tools such as Sweet Home 3D, LibreCAD and SketchUp's free web version let you draw walls to exact sizes and even place furniture in 3D. They take an evening or two to learn.

When to get house plans drawn for you

Drawing your own plan is ideal for working out your wishes. Consider getting help when:

  • You are about to spend real money. A designer can spot problems with layout, light, structure and circulation before they become expensive.
  • Your plot is narrow or unusual. Narrow plots like 25x50 or 30x60 ft need careful planning for stairs, light and parking.
  • You want to see it in 3D. A 2D sketch is hard to judge. A furnished 3D floor plan or a walkthrough video shows exactly how the home will feel.
  • You need it to look professional for your family, your builder or a bank.

Your hand-drawn plan is still valuable — it is the perfect brief. Send a photo of it with your measurements, and it can be turned into a clean, accurate custom house plan with 3D views.

A note on permits

Plans drawn by you or as concept designs are for planning and decision-making. For a building permit, a locally licensed architect or engineer prepares and stamps the final construction drawings, using your approved layout as the starting point.
